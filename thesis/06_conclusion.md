# 6. Conclusion

This thesis set out to flag unusual spending and income for people with irregular earnings, to explain each flag in plain language, and to ask whether a deep-learning detector is needed for it. The most important results came from examining the earlier work instead of extending it. The course-project loader had discarded 69% of the data, per-window scaling had thrown away how large each amount was, windows had mixed income with expenses, and the test used to show that the detector worked measured only whether it could detect impossible values. Once these were fixed and anomalies were injected realistically, the original detector was at chance.

Across three datasets (the original BudgetWise data, real bank data, and a simulation with labeled anomalies), the way an amount is scaled mattered more than the model. Scaling each amount relative to the user's typical amount made detection possible, and scaling it by how much its own history normally varies was the largest effect: on real bank expenses a one-line rule caught 43 ± 1% of 50% changes at a 5% false-positive rate, against 5% to 7% without that scaling and 14 ± 6% for the best autoencoder. A simple rule matched or beat an LSTM autoencoder on every dataset (for example 73.6 ± 4.1% against 41.0 ± 3.7% for tenfold spikes on BudgetWise), no detector found anomalies that break a pattern without being large in amount, and neither the real nor the synthetic data showed sequence dependence for a sequence model to use. For the case defined here, a point anomaly in amount, the deep model was not needed. That is a statement about these datasets, this model, and windows of five amounts, not about sequence models on data with richer temporal habits. Unexpectedly low income, the shortfall that matters most to a freelancer, was reliably detectable on BudgetWise but not on the real bank data.

For explanations, computing every number in code, letting a small local model only phrase the facts, and rejecting text that contradicts them gave output that was fully correct for 95% of 20 sampled flags in the final round, statistically indistinguishable from a plain template (100%). The design is worth keeping because it limits the damage of any single failure, but the model contributed phrasing and not accuracy, it was brittle to small prompt changes, and its characteristic errors (a true number used for the wrong thing, income called spending) are ones a value-checking filter cannot fully catch.

The practical lessons are to audit data before modeling, to check that an evaluation could actually fail, to compare against simple baselines and to test the intended use directly (here, income as well as expenses), to look for the scaling that fits how each history varies before choosing a model, and to report uncertainty and negative results. `[YOUR CALL: add one or two sentences on what you personally would do next.]`
