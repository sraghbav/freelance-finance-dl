# 6. Conclusion

This thesis set out to flag unusual spending and income for people with irregular earnings and to explain each flag in plain language. The most important results came from examining the earlier work instead of extending it. The course-project loader had discarded 69% of the data, per-window scaling had thrown away how large each amount was, windows had mixed income with expenses, and the test used to show that the detector worked measured only whether it could detect impossible values. Once these were fixed and anomalies were injected realistically, the original detector was at chance.

With amounts expressed relative to each user's typical amount, an LSTM autoencoder detected a charge ten times too large 41.0 ± 3.7% of the time at a 5% false-positive rate (five-fold cross-validation over users), but it was close to chance for changes of a factor of two or less and weak for drops. A one-line untrained rule did better for spikes (73.6 ± 4.1% when it knew the direction), and for unexpectedly low income it caught 94.7 ± 1.4% of tenfold drops against 8.3 ± 0.9% for the autoencoder. On a second, simulated dataset with real fraud labels, the best autoencoder at most tied the one-line rule (average precision 0.070 for both, with lower AUROC and recall), and both datasets lacked the time structure that a sequence model needs. For the case defined here, a point anomaly in amount, the learned detector is not justified on this evidence. That is a statement about these datasets, not about autoencoders on data with real habits.

For explanations, computing every number in code, letting a small local model only phrase the facts, and rejecting text that contradicts them gave output that was fully correct for 95% of 20 sampled flags in the final round, statistically indistinguishable from a plain template (100%). The design is worth keeping because it limits the damage of any single failure, but the model contributed phrasing and not accuracy, it was brittle to small prompt changes, and its characteristic errors (a true number used for the wrong thing, income called spending) are ones a value-checking filter cannot fully catch.
